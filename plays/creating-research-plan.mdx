## When?

**Before any round of calls** — discovery conversations or testing a prototype. If you can't say what you're trying to learn and from whom, you're not ready to schedule anything.

## Why?

**To make sure we build the right thing, in the right way — without wasting anyone's time.** While we may be experts in our product, we are not our users. A plan keeps the round honest: it forces the questions into the open, exposes your assumptions, and tells you when you're done.

## Step by step

### 1. One primary goal

Trying to hit two birds with one stone almost never works. Write the single thing this round must answer, and park the rest.

### 2. Identify your research questions

What are you trying to find out? Keep a record of all your research questions — broad or specific depending on your confidence level. They will shape your discussion guide so you get the answers you need without wasting time.

### 3. Check your assumptions

Be aware of your assumptions and biases; we all have them. Do you favour one idea, or believe users already know how to do something? Write them down so you can mitigate them in your research design — and aim to invalidate, not validate.

### 4. Decide who qualifies

Set criteria for whom you'd like to speak to and how many. Beware of only recruiting fans — seek the churned, the silent, and the workaround-builders too.

### 5. Define "done"

A round is done when you can answer the research questions with evidence, not when the calendar runs out. Five to eight sessions usually surfaces the patterns; plan to iterate rather than extend.
