> **Playbook vs Confluence** — this play covers the *tactics*: mindset, question craft, and listening technique. The *operational* side (scheduling, recruiting through CSMs, recording consent, Dovetail setup) lives in the Design Confluence.

## When?

**Early and continuously.** Before framing a problem, while exploring a space, and any time the team is designing from assumptions instead of evidence. If your last customer conversation was more than a few weeks ago, it's time.

## Why?

**We are not our users, and opinions don't survive contact with customers.** Discovery conversations are the cheapest way to find out what problems are real, how people work around them today, and what words they use to describe them — before any pixels or roadmap commitments exist. This is different from <PlayRef slug="testing-your-solutions" />: there you evaluate a solution; here you understand the problem space with no solution on the table.

**Aim to invalidate, not validate.** Validation carries every bias in the book; treat the conversation as a way to find out where you're wrong. Being wrong earlier is the whole point.

**Anyone can do this.** Research at Coveo is democratised — you don't need to be a designer or researcher to run these calls, and the design team is happy to pair with you and help you level up.

## Step by step

### 1. Decide what you're trying to learn

**One primary goal.** Trying to hit two birds with one stone almost never works. Write it down — "How do merchandisers decide a campaign is working?" beats "learn about reporting" — along with your assumptions, so the conversation can prove you wrong. And set criteria for *who* you want to speak to and why they qualify.

### 2. Ask about the past, not the future

People are unreliable predictors of their own behaviour. "Walk me through the last time you…" gets you facts; "would you use…" gets you politeness. Anchor every topic to a specific, recent, real example.

### 3. Ask open questions, then shut up

Start broad ("tell me about…"), follow the energy, and let silence do the work — people fill it with the honest second answer. Never pitch, never explain what you'd build, never rescue them from a struggle with your product; watch what the struggle teaches you.

### 4. Dig for the workaround

The spreadsheet next to your product is the roadmap. When someone describes a pain, ask what they do about it today — the effort of the workaround tells you how much the problem is actually worth.

### 5. Capture verbatim, synthesize later

Ask consent before recording — the first time, every time — and explain why you're recording. Take notes as close to a transcript as you can: don't interpret or summarize while capturing. Verbatim quotes avoid bias and are the evidence your later story runs on. Bring what you heard into a P.O.I.N.T. analysis or research synthesis — see <PlayRef slug="point-analysis" />.

### 6. Iterate between calls

You don't need the round to finish before acting. If a call surfaces something glaringly obvious, fix it; if something is unclear, ask specific questions in the next call. The round is a loop, not a batch.

## Common mistakes

1. **Pitching disguised as research** — the moment you explain your idea, you're collecting compliments, not evidence.
2. **Hypothetical and leading questions** — "would you", "could you imagine", or anything that invites a yes/no. Answers to imaginary questions are imaginary.
3. **Subjective comparisons** — "is this better/easier?" gets answers people can't explain. If they volunteer "easier", ask *why*.
4. **Only talking to fans** — your friendly customers say yes to calls *and* to everything else; seek the churned, the silent, the workaround-builders.
5. **One call, big conclusions** — patterns need repetition. Hold conclusions until you've heard the same thing three times.
