## When?

**After the research plan, before the first call.** The guide is what makes session five comparable to session one — and what stops a nervous facilitator from improvising leading questions.

## Why?

**A discussion guide is one of the most important steps to keep your research fair, unbiased and consistent.** It converts research questions into things you can actually say and do in a session, and it frees you to listen instead of thinking about what to ask next.

## Step by step

### 1. Set the scene

Write the opening: what the session is about, that you're testing the idea and not them, that honest reactions — including thinking out loud — are the most helpful thing they can do.

### 2. Turn research questions into a script and tasks

For each research question, write the prompts or tasks that will answer it. For prototypes, think about how participants will navigate and where each answer should come from. For discovery, anchor prompts to the recent past — "walk me through the last time you…".

### 3. Order it from broad to specific

Start open, narrow down. Early specificity contaminates everything after it: once you've shown your interest in a feature, every later answer bends toward it.

### 4. Strip the bias out

Read every question hunting for leads: anything answerable with yes/no, anything hypothetical ("would you…"), anything comparative-subjective ("is this easier?"). Rewrite them open and behavioural.

### 5. Pilot it

Run the guide once on a colleague. You'll find the confusing task, the missing question, and the section that takes three times longer than planned — before it costs you a real participant.
