## When?

**At direction moments.** Entering a new problem space, a yearly or quarterly strategy reset, or after discovery surfaces an opportunity too big for one roadmap line. The tell: the team ships increment after increment, but nobody can say what it's all adding up to — or you need executives, partners, and future hires to *believe* in a destination, not just approve a plan.

## Why?

**A vision deck gets nods; a visiontype gets believers.** A visiontype is a prototype of the product vision — the term is [SVPG's](https://www.svpg.com/the-power-of-visiontypes/) — and its power is that it lets people *see* a future that could be, not read bullet points about it. Steve Jobs didn't copy the Xerox PARC demo; the demo made a future imaginable, and Apple spent years of discovery making it real. That's the mechanism: alignment first, innovation after.

**The vision is where discovery starts, not where it ends.** A little problem discovery goes *into* a strong vision; the vast majority of the work — and nearly all the innovation — comes *after* it, as solution discovery and delivery. The visiontype's job is only to make everyone want to do that work in the same direction.

## What do you need?

A PM + designer pair with a real opinion about the future, the evidence breadcrumbs from discovery (customer quotes, journey pain, market shifts), one to two weeks, and a prototype medium fast enough to build a future — which today means <PlayRef slug="prototyping-with-agents" />.

## Step by step

### 1. Anchor on a customer's future moment, not a feature list

Pick the two or three moments in a future user's day that would prove the vision. Write them as a story before touching any tool — if the story doesn't hold as prose, no amount of prototype polish will save it.

### 2. Choose the flag distance

Far enough that current constraints don't apply; near enough to stay believable. Three years of ambition beats ten years of science fiction — a visiontype that reads as fantasy aligns no one.

### 3. Build it like it's real

Real components, real-feeling data, plausible names and numbers. It's theatre: polish exactly what the story walks through, and fake everything else without apology. Nobody will click where the script doesn't go.

### 4. Wrap it in the narrative

The demo *is* a story: today's pain first, then the future moment. Rehearse the walkthrough — a visiontype presented badly is just a weird unshippable prototype.

### 5. Show it to recruit, not to test

Playbacks with leadership, teams, and candidates. You're not running usability sessions; you're watching for the alignment signal — "when can we have this?" — and capturing what it provokes.

### 6. Mine it for the roadmap

Decompose the vision into opportunities and bets. The visiontype earns its keep when, months later, a <PlayRef slug="shaping-workshop" /> opens with "remember the visiontype? This is the first slice."

## Common mistakes

1. **Treating it as a commitment** — it's a compass, not a contract. Date-stamping the flag turns alignment into scheduled disappointment.
2. **Falling in love with the enabling technology** — the classic vision-killer: betting the future on tech you love before discovering whether it can carry a product. Love the problem instead.
3. **Confusing it with a first release** — nobody builds the visiontype. You discover your way toward it, slice by slice.
4. **Vision without delivery** — sharing a vision recruits believers, but if you can't discover and deliver against it, you've just briefed your competitors. Xerox made the visiontype; Apple shipped the vision.
5. **Playing it safe** — if it looks like next quarter's release, it's a roadmap slide wearing a costume. It should be slightly uncomfortable to present.

## What next?

Decompose it: the riskiest beliefs inside the vision become <PlayRef slug="provocations" /> to test, and the first slice becomes a <PlayRef slug="shaping-workshop" />. Revisit the visiontype on a cadence — a flag nobody looks at is just a stick in the ground.
