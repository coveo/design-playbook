## When?

**When you need breadth, not speed.** A strategic, cross-team project where one or two loud voices shouldn't steer the outcome, and you want several people saying the same thing before you change direction. It shines across time zones: when London has lunch as Montreal wakes up, no slot works for everyone.

Not every crit should go async. If you need a decision this afternoon, or only two people's opinions, book the room.

## Why?

**Nobody has to find a slot.**

- **People think before they speak.** Introverts and non-designers get time to look properly, with no jargon thrown at them and no pressure to say the "right" thing on the spot.
- **Opinions stay independent.** Nobody waits for their turn and ends up agreeing with whoever spoke first.
- **The room gets bigger.** Anyone in the channel can follow along, and a 5-minute video is short enough that even leadership might watch it.
- **Less context switching.** People review when they have focus time, not in the gap between two meetings.

## What do you need?

**An audience** of 6–8 people, cross-functional on purpose: a fellow designer, a design system designer, a PM from another team, an engineer, someone customer-facing, and leadership where it matters. Diverse opinions keep you out of the designer echo chamber. More people will join unasked, so make everything readable by anyone in the channel.

**A board** in Miro or Figma, **a short video walkthrough**, a shared channel for crits, and **5 working days**.

## Step by step

### 1. Build the board (before day 1)

Start with the problem: an example or two, and a link to the initiative. Add what research told you (interview findings, analytics, competitor examples) and the one question this crit must answer. Then the critique itself: a user flow, A/B/C variants, specific jobs to be done.

Lay it out as a flow on the board. Prototypes are great, but people can't comment on them or, more importantly, on each other's comments.

### 2. Set the rules (before day 1)

Say how you want feedback. Ask for comments, not sticky notes: they're easy to drop, and threads keep the conversation going, both in the moment and when someone gets a notification that a colleague disagrees.

### 3. Record a walkthrough (before day 1)

5–10 minutes at double speed. Walk through the problem, the research and the solution as if the video were the only thing people will see. A Miro or screen-recording plugin is enough; post the video with the message or pin it to the board.

### 4. Check access, then share out (day 1)

> **Before you post** — everyone on the list has commenter access to the board, and the video is recorded and viewable. Finding out on day three is the most common way these crits stall.

In Miro, open **Share** and set the company to **Commenter**, so anyone who follows the link can comment, not just the people you tagged:

![Miro's Share dialog with board access for the Coveo company set to Commenter](covers/async-crit-board-access.png)

Then post to the channel. Tag your audience, give the problem and the solution in a sentence each, say exactly what to do, and end with the video and the deadline:

![Example shareout message in Slack: an async crit on campaign scheduling that tags the audience, states the problem and solution, lists three things to do, gives the deadline and attaches the walkthrough video](covers/async-crit-shareout.png)

### 5. Nudge half-way (day 3)

Nothing much happens in the first two days; people have lives. Half-way through, reply in the thread, tag whoever hasn't taken part yet, and ask them to say if they can't. Expect someone to ask for an extension.

![Example half-way nudge in the Slack thread: replies from people who are done, then a reminder that tags the three who haven't taken part yet, restates the deadline and asks them to say if they can't](covers/async-crit-followup.png)

### 6. Review with your quadfecta (day 5)

Go through the comments and the themes together. What did people like? What did they hate? What needs unplanned exploration? Which variant won?

### 7. Close the loop (day 5)

Thank everyone in the thread and share what you're keeping, what you're revisiting, what needs follow-up, and what happens next. People who see their comments land are the ones who say yes to your next crit.

![Example closing message in the Slack thread: a thank-you with a summary of what's being kept, what's being revisited, what needs follow-up, and the next steps](covers/async-crit-close.png)

## Common mistakes

1. **A two-day deadline** — people need time to actually do this.
2. **Finding access problems on day three** — check before you share.
3. **A 20-minute video** — nobody is watching that at any speed.
4. **No clear question** — the feedback scatters across everything.
5. **Skipping the nudge** — silence isn't consent; it's usually a full inbox.
6. **Going quiet at the end** — no summary, no second crit.
